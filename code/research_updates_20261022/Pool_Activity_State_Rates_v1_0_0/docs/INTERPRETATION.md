# Absolute gaps, proportional gaps and talk denominators

The latest criticism identifies a real distinction. The existing result establishes a smaller **absolute** desert gap among the most active observed choosers. It does not by itself establish less proportional state dependence or a less selective preference.

If p_F and p_L are the competitive and clearly-worse grant rates and R=p_F/p_L, then

**p_F − p_L = p_L(R − 1).**

At a fixed relative contrast R, a smaller p_L gives a smaller percentage-point gap. For example, hypothetical rates of 4% versus 1% give a 3 pp gap and a ratio of 4; rates of 2% versus 0.5% give a 1.5 pp gap and the same ratio. Lower overall kindness alone does not mathematically force a smaller gap without a maintained relation between state rates; the fixed-R example shows why the absolute gap is insufficient.

The runner checks overall rates within the exact activity groups used in the previous comparison, then reports both state rates and their ratio. A ratio compares relative state dependence on this chosen scale; it is not a measure of an individual preference parameter or the causal effect of activity. Group composition, experience and other correlates of observed activity remain possible sources of heterogeneity.

## What the extra table does

The groups remain light (2–6), medium (7–16) and heavy (17+) competitive-plus-clearly-worse opportunities, among the same 1,982,345 choosers observed in both states. No account is excluded for having no grants. The primary small table gives each chooser equal weight; companion tables reproduce the original FE and inverse-count weighting and provide the raw pooled rates.

For each weighting, the table reports the overall binary-sample grant rate, competitive rate, clearly-worse rate, absolute gap and competitive/clearly-worse rate ratio. A separate column standardizes the state mix to 50/50. The ratio of the heavy group's RR to the light group's RR compares the proportional contrast directly, with an interval. All these are descriptive conditional comparisons.

With common chooser weights in both state rates, their difference equals the previous corresponding within-chooser coefficient. Raw opportunity-pooled state rates use different chooser weights in the two states, so their difference generally is not the FE coefficient. Keeping these definitions separate avoids attributing a state-composition change to within-person selectivity.

## Paper wording about weighting

Suggested methods language:

> The baseline regressions are estimated at the opportunity level, giving each included opportunity equal weight. With chooser fixed effects, the minimal binary desert coefficient averages observed within-chooser state differences with weights F_i L_i/(F_i+L_i). It therefore gives greater weight to choosers with more opportunities and substantial exposure to both states. Reweighting checks describe the sensitivity of that minimal contrast to the chooser weights.

“Game-weighted” is acceptable shorthand for the observation-level fitting convention only if the relevant games are identified as qualifying opportunities. It is not the exact chooser-level weighting formula. For adjusted models, residualizing the other controls changes the information weights; the simple binary formula need not apply.

This add-on does not change or reestimate the paper's adjusted results. Their interpretation should not be replaced by a claim of robustness inferred from this minimal binary check. The new table also does not retrospectively establish that the old 2.17 and new 2.82 pp estimates are the same estimand.
